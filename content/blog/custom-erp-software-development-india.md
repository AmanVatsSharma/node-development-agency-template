You probably need a custom ERP only if your way of running operations is part of what makes your business competitive, or if a packaged tool would need so many workarounds that you would be paying for customisation anyway. For standard accounting, inventory and billing, a packaged product is almost always faster and cheaper. This guide explains how to tell the difference, what drives cost and timeline, and how custom ERP projects go wrong.

## What an ERP actually covers

ERP stands for enterprise resource planning. In practice it means one shared system and one shared database for the functions that otherwise live in separate spreadsheets and tools, so that sales, stock and money all agree with each other. A typical Indian SME ERP includes some mix of:

- **Finance and GST** — invoicing, purchase bills, ledgers, GST returns data, e-invoicing and e-way bill support where applicable
- **Inventory and warehousing** — stock by location, batches or serial numbers, reorder levels, stock transfers
- **Procurement** — purchase requests, approvals, vendor management, goods receipt
- **Sales and CRM** — quotations, orders, dispatch, customer history (see our [CRM services](/pages/crm) if sales is your main pain)
- **Production** — bills of material, work orders, job work, wastage tracking
- **HR and payroll** — attendance, leave, payroll inputs
- **Reporting** — margin per order, per customer and per product; ageing; stock valuation

Most ERP projects fail because of scope and adoption, not because of technology. The goal is not to build every module. It is to replace the handful of manual hand-offs that cost you money today.

## Off-the-shelf vs custom ERP

There is a large middle ground between "buy Tally and add spreadsheets" and "build everything from scratch". Established options include Tally with add-ons, Zoho, ERPNext, Odoo and SAP Business One. The right choice depends on how different your process is from the standard one.

| Factor | Off-the-shelf / open-source ERP | Custom-built ERP |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first use | Weeks | Typically 2–4 months for the first module |
| Fit with your process | You adapt to the product | The product adapts to you |
| Licence cost | Per user, recurring | None, but you fund development |
| Upfront cost | Lower | Higher |
| Flexibility | Limited by the product's design | High, if the architecture is sound |
| Vendor dependence | On the product vendor | On whoever maintains your code, unless you own it |
| Ongoing cost | Subscriptions and customisation | Maintenance and enhancements |

A sensible path for many businesses is to start with a packaged or open-source ERP and customise it. Move to a custom build only when the gap between the product and your process becomes the main cost.

## Signs you have outgrown spreadsheets and Tally

- The same order or invoice is typed into two or three places.
- Month-end reconciliation takes days and still does not match the bank or stock.
- Physical stock rarely equals system stock, and nobody trusts either number.
- Approvals happen over WhatsApp calls and nobody can see who approved what.
- You cannot answer "what is our real margin on this customer?" without a day of manual work.
- Matching purchase data against GST returns is a manual monthly exercise.
- Adding a second warehouse, branch or company would break your current setup.

If three or more of these are true, you have an operations problem, and an ERP is one way to solve it.

## When custom is justified (and when it is not)

**Custom tends to be justified when:**

- Your workflow is a genuine competitive advantage, for example a specific production, quoting or dispatch process that competitors cannot easily copy.
- You need deep integrations that packaged tools handle poorly, such as machines, weighbridges, biometric devices, B2B customer portals or marketplace feeds.
- You have already tried a packaged product and are spending more on customisation than on the licence.
- You need multi-entity, multi-currency or sector-specific compliance logic that is awkward to configure.

**Custom is usually the wrong choice when:**

- Your needs are standard accounting, inventory and billing.
- You have no one internally who owns the process and can make decisions quickly.
- You want a fixed price for a loosely defined scope. Vague scope plus a fixed price produces either a bad product or a dispute.

## What drives the cost

Cost follows scope, and scope is made of a few predictable pieces.

| Cost driver | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Number of modules | Each module has its own screens, rules and reports |
| User roles and permissions | Approvals, branch-level access and audit trails add real design work |
| Integrations | GST e-invoicing, payment gateways, courier APIs, biometric devices, existing software |
| Data migration | Cleaning and importing years of masters and opening balances is often underestimated |
| Reports and dashboards | A system is judged by its reports; define them early |
| Mobile or offline use | Warehouse and field teams often need phone-friendly or offline-capable screens |
| Security and audit | Activity logs, backups, access controls, data retention |
| Training and rollout | Adoption work is part of the project, not an afterthought |

**Illustrative planning ranges, not a quote:** in the Indian market, a focused single-department system such as inventory plus billing is commonly planned in the range of a few lakh to the low tens of lakhs of rupees. A multi-module ERP for a mid-size manufacturer or distributor is a larger programme, often from the tens of lakhs to a crore or more, depending on integrations and the number of users and sites. Treat these as orders of magnitude for budgeting conversations. Real numbers come from a scoped estimate, and any estimate you receive before discovery is mostly guesswork. ## A realistic timeline

| Phase | Typical duration | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery and process mapping | 2–4 weeks | Interview users, map current flows, agree what is in and out of scope |
| Design and data model | 2–4 weeks | Screens, roles, reports, integrations, database design |
| Build, module by module | 8–20 weeks | Work in short cycles; release one module to real users at a time |
| Data migration and testing | 3–6 weeks | Rehearse imports, run user acceptance tests, fix issues |
| Training and go-live | 2–4 weeks | Train by role, run in parallel with the old process if needed |
| Support after launch | 4–8 weeks | Fast fixes while real data and real habits settle in |

These are indicative ranges. A phased plan that puts the first module in front of real users within the first couple of months beats a single "big bang" launch at month nine, because you learn what is wrong while it is still cheap to change.

## Risks and how to avoid them

1. **Scope creep.** Fix scope per phase. Put new ideas into a backlog and decide each phase what moves in.
2. **Underestimated data migration.** Start rehearsing imports early. Bad master data (duplicate customers, inconsistent item codes) will surface the moment you load it.
3. **No internal owner.** Name one person who can make process decisions. Without that person, requirements stall and every opinion becomes a change request.
4. **Reports as an afterthought.** List the ten reports management actually uses during discovery, and design the data around them.
5. **Low adoption.** Train by role, run the old and new processes side by side for a short period, and track usage. A working ERP that people bypass is a failed project.
6. **Lock-in.** Put source-code ownership, documentation and a mainstream technology stack in the contract, so you can change vendors without rewriting.

## FAQ

### How much does custom ERP software cost in India?

It depends on modules, users, integrations and data migration. Small focused systems and full multi-module ERPs sit in very different ranges. Ask for a phased estimate after a discovery phase rather than a single number up front, and be wary of fixed quotes given before anyone has mapped your process.

### Should I start with ERPNext, Odoo or Zoho instead of building custom?

Often yes. Starting from an established product and customising it gets you live sooner. Build custom when the changes needed are so large that you are fighting the product instead of using it.

### Can a custom ERP handle GST e-invoicing and e-way bills?

Yes, generally through integration with the government's e-invoicing and e-way bill systems via an authorised provider. Whether these apply to you depends on your turnover and transaction type, so confirm requirements with your chartered accountant before design.

### How soon will we see value?

With a phased plan, the first module (often inventory, billing or order management) can be in real use within roughly two to three months. Be cautious of plans where nothing is usable until the end.

### Who should own the source code?

You should. Make that explicit in the contract, along with documentation and handover of credentials and hosting accounts.

### What happens after launch?

Plan for support and enhancements. A common budgeting rule of thumb is to reserve a portion of the build cost each year for maintenance, hosting and improvements. Confirm what is included before you sign.
